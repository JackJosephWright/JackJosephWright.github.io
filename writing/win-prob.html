What predicts winning in Magic: 5.1M games

Data
7 sets, 5,109,204 games, 31.4M card episodes (public 17lands exports)
Method
Logistic regression, 5-fold grouped by draft, card encodings re-fit inside each fold
Question
Does knowing which cards are on the board beat knowing only the shape of it?

Rearranging the same numbers beat adding new ones

The largest single improvement in the whole study came from changing no data at all. Card values summarised as an average across two zones gave one result. The same values, described instead as a distribution — total, best, worst, spread, and how many were actually known — across three zones gave a 71% larger improvement.

An average destroys the shape of a zone. One bomb among ten worthless tokens averages out to mediocre. The spread matters, the best card matters, and "measurably weak" and "never seen before" are different states that an average silently merges.

You cannot read card quality off the card

If rarity, mana cost and card type predicted how good a card is, none of this measurement would be necessary — you could compute it for any card ever printed. They don't.

Differences between cards are 98.8% real signal rather than noise. Of that, rarity, cost and type explain 3%. Guessing that every card is exactly average does better than using them. Two Final Fantasy rares — both three mana, both colourless, identical on every attribute you can see on the card — win at 41.4% and 63.6%.

Rarity is nearly flat, and not even in the right order: mythics beat commons beat rares beat uncommons. Good decks contain good cards, and that effect swamps rarity entirely.

The correction I had to publish

An earlier version of this work reported that rarity, cost and type recovered 93% of the signal — the opposite conclusion. Both numbers were arithmetically correct. The first was 93% of total prediction accuracy, in a model where card quality is only about a tenth of the total; the second is 3% of card quality. The first denominator makes anything look good, because most of the prediction comes from life totals and board counts regardless.

Nothing static can price an attack

The most useful result is a limit. Grading the model on whether it recovers the real value of a known-good play:

PlaySimple model+16 carefully chosen features
Removal spell29%120%
Attack~31%~31%

Sixteen more features moved attacking not at all. Removal went from barely credited to fully credited.

The reason is clean. A removal spell's entire value is written into the board you can see afterwards — a creature is gone. An attack's value mostly isn't. It lives in damage that matters three turns later, in the blocks the opponent now has to consider, in a race that hasn't resolved. A snapshot of a position is structurally blind to that, and no amount of extra snapshot detail fixes it.

Two smaller findings

Ending your own turn is worse than ending theirs. All else equal, the same board is worth measurably less at the end of your turn — you're tapped out and it's about to be their move. Obvious once stated, and invisible to every earlier model here, because none of them recorded whose turn the snapshot came from.

Card values conditioned on the wrong event teach hoarding. Measured by whether you were holding a card, values climb steadily with turn number — holding cards on turn 10 correlates with winning. True as correlation, useless as advice: still holding cards late means the game went long, which usually means you were ahead. Feed that to a bot and it learns to sit on its hand. Measuring instead by whether the card was cast conditions on having done something, and the curve goes flat.
