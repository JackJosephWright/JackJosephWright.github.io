Data validation on a 5.1M-game Magic dataset

Data
Public 17lands replay exports. 7 sets, 5,109,204 games, 31.4M card episodes
Role
Sole engineer
Output
A dataset contract documenting 8 traps, each with the measurement that establishes it
Tests
137 unit tests across five builder modules

Why this exists

The dataset is a per-game snapshot log: for every turn of every game, what was in each player's hand, on each board, what was cast, what died. It is generous and well documented, and it is also full of encodings that look like one thing and mean another.

Every item below silently produced, or nearly produced, a wrong number in this project. None of them raise an error. Each is recorded with the measurement that established it and the sample size, so the claim can be rechecked rather than trusted.

Trap 1: the last row of every game is fake, but only on the draw

The final snapshot in game order is a zeroed "game over" record — no cards, no board, both life totals at zero — carrying the game's real outcome. Because snapshot ordering depends on who went first, it lands on the opponent's slot when you are on the play and on your slot when you are on the draw.

On the play0.00% (5 of 262,385)0.00%
On the draw5.04% (13,305 of 263,842)21.7%

These are zero-state rows labelled with a win or a loss, and they are asymmetric by arm, so they do not average out. Past the end of a game, life columns also read 0.0 for both players forever, so an unclamped loop sees both players dead for the tail of every game.

Trap 2: some card IDs are not cards

Real card identifiers start at 6873. Below that are engine sentinels. The one that mattered is -1, meaning "hidden or unknown card." It is rare in most sets and roughly fifty times more common in one of them.

A different sentinel represents the opponent's face-down creature. It accounts for 8.95% of opponent battlefield creature entries, appears in attack and block columns, and never appears in any cast column — which is how its meaning was established rather than assumed.

Tokens are not sentinels. They are real high-numbered identifiers that resolve in the card index, and they make up around 13% of creature-zone entries.

Trap 3: zones are assigned by current characteristics, not printed type

A face-down creature logs its true identifier in the creature column. So 3.91% of your creature entries are printed lands and 1.35% are printed instants and sorceries. "On the battlefield" for a sorcery means it was a face-down 2/2, not that its effect was in play.

The consequence is that the land and creature columns are not disjoint by identifier: 335 of 481 distinct land identifiers in one set also appear in the creature column. Any code that unions zones into a set collapses about 0.9% of entries.

Trap 4: split cards use different IDs in different columns

Cast columns record the identifier of the half that was cast. Hand and battlefield columns record the identifier of the whole card. Without canonicalisation the two never join.

Trap 5: one card is many IDs

Identifiers are per art printing. Keying on them splits a single card into several rows with separate sample sizes — a basic land landed at both extremes of one ranking at the same time. Folding to card name raised the share of between-card variance that is real signal from 33% to 51%.

The same applies to tokens. One set has five different Clue identifiers spanning eight points of win rate. Across thirteen multi-variant token names, variation within a name is 5.36 points against 8.89 points between names, so roughly a third of apparent token value is really "which card made it."

Trap 6: about 6% of the opponent's board has no identity

Just under 6% of opponent board entries carry no usable identifier, peaking at 7.45% around turn five. These are real objects that attack and block. Dropping them undercounts the opponent's board; looking them up in a card-value table is undefined.

They also carry no printed stats, which means printed power and toughness sums undercount the opponent's board about 3.5 points more often than they undercount yours. A model comparing the two boards inherits that asymmetry.

Trap 7: negative combat damage is correct

The combat damage column occasionally goes negative, with values as low as −78. It is not corruption. It is a signed net life change from combat, so negative means that player gained life — lifelink. The segment life change matches the column exactly 92.5% of the time overall and 95.3% excluding each game's final segment.

Clamping negatives to zero, which is the obvious defensive move, deletes the lifelink signal entirely.

Trap 8: ability columns are not card IDs

Ability firings are logged with their own identifier space. Not one of 547,448 firings in one set coincides with any card identifier the player held, cast, or had on board. The mapping from ability to source card had to be built empirically by co-occurrence, which resolves 83% of firings to a single source at high confidence, and it has to be rebuilt per set.

Three bugs that reached a shipped table

These were found by auditing the schema against what the builder assumed rather than by anything failing.

BugEffect
Engine sentinels emitted as real cards The -1 "card" shipped with 14,884 games at a 0.730 win rate against a 0.550 base — an 18-point phantom outlier. It was also feeding the shrinkage priors of every real card around it, so the damage was not confined to its own row.
Split-face casts never joining 7.13% of one set's cast volume orphaned. Fixing the join recovered 904,368 cast-games across 23 cards.
Instants and sorceries in the battlefield table A spell cannot be on the battlefield; 100% of these arrived via the face-down manifest path in Trap 3. Their between-card variance measured 0–11% real signal — almost pure noise being published as data.

The fixes were applied at aggregation time. Because the per-set intermediate counts were complete and correct, the table rebuilt from cache with no re-scan of the source data.

Shrinkage, and why one parameter was not enough

Per-card win rates at the turn level are thin — a few hundred observations per cell — so raw rates are dominated by sampling noise at the extremes. Two empirical-Bayes estimators handle this, both fit by method of moments because the accumulators are exact integers and no likelihood surface needs searching.

Card win rates shrink in two stages: each card toward its set's base rate, then each card-turn cell toward that card's own shrunk rate. The concentration parameter for the second stage is fit on within-card deviations. Fitting it on raw cell rates would fold in the much larger between-card variance, overstate the spread, and under-shrink every cell.

The parameter is also fit separately per quantity, and the two stages move in opposite directions:

QuantityCard-level concentrationCell-level concentration
Card in hand226–30960–104
Card on board47–9479–128
Card cast this turn58–88126–188

Cards differ far more from each other once you condition on casting or on survival, so those quantities shrink less at card level. Within a single card, the turn dimension carries more real spread for a card sitting in hand than for one being cast, whose observations cluster near its mana cost. A single shared parameter would over-shrink one quantity while under-shrinking another.

The survivorship gap, predicted before it was measured

Win rates conditioned on a card being on the board are survivorship filtered — the card is only observed in games where it lived long enough to be seen. The size of that effect was registered as a prediction before measuring, so a null or negative result would have indicated a bug in the pipeline rather than an interesting finding.

Paired on the same card, set, and turn across 15,504 cells, on-board win rates run 3.6 points above in-hand, positive in 65.8% of pairs. That is the confound, and it is the reason board presence is usable as a state readout and not as a signal about whether casting the card was a good idea.

Measuring how long a card survives

A separate table measures fragility: how many turn cycles a card lasts after it appears, capped at four. Roughly 40% of observations are censored by the game ending first, and the censoring time is averaged in rather than modelled, so this is not a Kaplan-Meier restricted mean and is labelled accordingly. The bias is known and stated: cards that tend to arrive late read as shorter-lived than they are.

Between-card differences are 98–99.9% real signal across all seven sets. The correlations are mechanically sensible, which is the main evidence that the measurement works: survival is negatively correlated with mana cost and with power, and positively correlated with toughness exceeding power. Fragile cards are large cheap-to-answer attackers; durable cards are defensive statlines.

One caveat found while writing this up, and worth stating because the shipped table does not enforce it: the death columns only ever contain creatures, so any row whose printed type is not a creature can never have an episode closed. About a quarter of rows — tokens, lands, spells, artifacts — are structurally unkillable, and their "survival" is pure censoring time. They accumulate at the durable end of the ranking and have to be filtered out before the table is read.

Proving a join is right using an invariant

A newer dataset pairs each of an expert player's turns with the board state immediately before it — 10.8M turn cycles drawn from 1.18M games played by highly ranked drafters. Building it requires selecting the correct preceding snapshot, and that rule differs depending on who went first. Getting it wrong is silent: the rows still look plausible, and a wrong join leaks the turn's own actions into the state that is supposed to precede them.

The check is an invariant rather than a spot inspection. When your turn T begins, you have completed exactly T−1 turns, so the pre-state can never contain more than T−1 lands. The three candidate join rules predict different values at turn two: the correct one predicts exactly 1, and the two wrong ones predict 0 and 2. Measured over 800,000 turn cycles, both arms read exactly 1.00, at 100.0% exact agreement.

A second check falls out of the same measurement. By later turns the invariant loosens as players miss land drops, and the decay is larger for the player who went first — which is what card advantage predicts, since the player on the draw has seen an extra card. A swapped join reverses the sign of that gap, so the decay pattern independently confirms the join.

A finding that killed its own deliverable

The expert dataset was meant to produce a benchmark: situations where experts overwhelmingly agree on the action, used as a regression test for the agent. A first version emitted 616 such questions.

Measuring the agreement distribution across 840,796 expert decisions showed why that benchmark was not worth keeping:

Expert agreementShare of decisionsDominant action
80%+11.4%Develop, or attack with everything
60–80%37.6%Develop
40–60%39.9%Partial attack
Under 40%11.1%Partial attack

Only 11.4% of decision mass sits where experts agree, and that band contains no partial attacks at all. Attacking with everything or holding back entirely is usually forced by the board. Choosing which three of five creatures attack is the actual judgement, and it never concentrates. A benchmark filtered for consensus collects precisely the decisions that were never in doubt. The deliverable was deleted, with the measurement kept as the reason.

What saturates and what does not

Sampling more of one set buys card coverage quickly and state coverage never. At 10% of a single set, 256 of 275 cards already have enough observations; the remaining 90% of the data adds seven more. State coverage does not converge — at the full million games, sixteen previously unseen board configurations still appear per ten thousand decisions.

The practical consequence is that breadth across formats beats depth within one. Doubling the sample of a single set buys two more cards and about two points of decision coverage. A new set buys roughly 275 new cards and a different metagame.

Method notes

  • The exports are chronological. Sampling the first N rows trains on a format's opening days, when it is unsolved and players are worse. Sample by hashing the draft identifier.
  • Per-turn denominators are games that reached that turn. One set has 1,011,949 games reaching turn 1 and 148,907 reaching turn 12; using total games makes every per-turn average decay toward zero and look like a real decline.
  • Group by draft, not by row, when splitting for validation. Games from one draft share a deck.
  • Aggregates hide per-card effects. Every two-mana creature's board value decays with turn, and the curve pooled across all two-drops is flat.
  • State the denominator of a percentage. "Face attributes explain 93%" and "face attributes explain 3%" were both true of the same data, against different denominators, and the first version of this work published the wrong one.
  • The shipped tables publish raw counts and fitted priors alongside the shrunk values, so a consumer can re-fit shrinkage inside their own training fold. Using the published shrunk value directly as a model feature leaks.
